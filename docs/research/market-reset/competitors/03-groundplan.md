# Groundplan — direct verdict

**Verdict (research date: 26 September 2026): Groundplan is a credible Australian incumbent and a serious distribution benchmark, but not a direct proof that AI estimating can command KindAI’s planned premium tiers.** Its moat is a focused, low-friction takeoff workflow, strong human onboarding/support, visible trade-specific language, and integrations into the systems Australian contractors already use. Its public offer is materially more accessible than KindAI’s planned positioning: the Simpro marketplace lists **A$99/month** pay-as-you-go Groundplan and **A$79/month** Count Assist, with the listing last updated 21 July 2025 [9]. Groundplan’s own site currently confirms a **7-day unrestricted trial**, free trade-expert demonstration and free training, but its pricing table did not load during this review [2].

For KindAI, Groundplan proves that Australian trade buyers will pay for a specialised plan-to-quantity workflow and that integration-led distribution is practical. It does **not** prove demand for AI takeoffs, a willingness to pay A$149–A$1,499/month, or product-market fit for KindAI. The most defensible response is not to copy the cheap trial. It is to sell a narrow, premium outcome—reviewable estimates from Australian plans, with transparent assumptions and export into the buyer’s existing workflow—while using a short, guided evaluation rather than unlimited free AI.

## What Groundplan sells

Groundplan describes itself as cloud-based software to “design, measure & estimate your plans online.” It targets subcontractors, general contractors and suppliers across electrical, plumbing, security, fire, painting, roofing, concrete, plasterboard, HVAC, audio visual, landscaping and building [1]. Its demonstrated workflow is deliberately simple: upload PDF/JPG/PNG/TIFF plans, count items or measure lengths/areas on-screen, generate a bill of quantities, then export to Excel or a connected job-management/accounting system [1] [3].

The official product demo reinforces the same positioning rather than presenting a broad construction operating system. The narrator says Groundplan is “built for any business in the trade industry that uses floor plans to takeoff and estimate,” and shows plan upload, point counting, polygon/linear measurement, automatic line-item calculations and export [13]. The video shows a 2D workflow; it does not demonstrate BIM/model extraction or AI-generated takeoffs. The demonstration is marketing evidence, not independent proof of accuracy or time savings.

Groundplan’s public proof points are substantial but vendor-reported. Its homepage says “8,000+ contractors” across Australia, New Zealand and beyond, and displays **8,650+ happy users, 412,700+ projects completed and 6.2 million+ plans estimated** [1]. Those figures should be treated as self-reported adoption indicators, not independently audited customer counts. LinkedIn lists the company as founded in 2013, privately held, headquartered in Surfers Paradise, with 11–50 employees [10].

The product also extends beyond measurement. Groundplan promotes reusable templates/formulas, branded PDF plan documents, collaboration, real-time project access, bill-of-quantities outputs and **Count Assist**, an add-on that automates symbol counting for electrical, security and fire plans [1] [3]. The site’s language is outcome-oriented—faster quoting, less manual work and better control of materials/labour—but it does not publish a controlled benchmark against manual estimating.

## Pricing, trial and entry-offer implications

Groundplan’s current official pricing page was partially unavailable: it displayed “Free for 7 days” and an error stating that the pricing table could not load [2]. It does clearly state that the seven-day trial has unrestricted feature access, a trade-expert demonstration is available during the trial, billing details are required to continue after the trial, and customers can cancel at any time without lock-in contracts [2]. The official homepage repeats “Start Your 7-Day Free Trial” and says expert onboarding is included [1].

The most concrete public Australian price is the official Simpro Marketplace listing. It shows Groundplan Monthly at **A$99/month**, Groundplan Yearly at **A$990/year**, Count Assist Monthly at **A$79/month**, and Count Assist Yearly at **A$790/year**; it warns that the figures are display-only, should be checked with the partner, and were last updated **21 July 2025** [9]. This is stale relative to the research date, so it is a price anchor rather than a guaranteed 26 September 2026 quote. Independent Australian review site The Techy Side reported a starting price “around $75/month,” a seven-day full-access trial, and no lock-in in its review dated 6 March 2026 [7]. Software Advice Australia also lists no free version, a free trial and a starting price of USD75/month [8].

Groundplan therefore uses a classic low-friction land motion: free unrestricted evaluation, free human training, monthly cancellation and a low public entry anchor. This is a meaningful contrast with KindAI’s removal of the A$9 trial. The lesson is not that KindAI must match A$99. The lesson is that premium pricing requires an explicit reason to switch from a known, supported workflow. A bounded KindAI evaluation should show a complete representative estimate, not merely expose a few AI credits. The gate should be evidence quality and buyer review, with minimal free AI usage and a clear paid conversion path.

Groundplan’s pricing also reveals a packaging risk for KindAI. Groundplan separates core takeoff from Count Assist, so buyers can understand what they are paying for. KindAI’s A$149 Sole Tradie, A$450 Pro and A$1,499 Mid-Tier Builder tiers should similarly make the unit of value legible—projects, reviewed estimates, users, or workflow throughput—rather than only presenting a large monthly AI allowance. Any comparison against Groundplan must disclose that Groundplan’s current public seat/usage limits were not visible on the official pricing page during this review.

## Features, integrations and positioning

Groundplan’s core differentiation is purpose-built simplicity. It claims to be “the industry standard for estimating professionals” [1], but the evidence supporting that phrase is its own customer and usage claims, not market-share data. The product’s practical positioning is clearer: browser-based takeoff for trade businesses that need to quantify plans and turn quantities into quotes. The official pages repeatedly emphasise ease, remote access, templates, support and exports [1] [3].

Its integration surface is strategically important in Australia. Groundplan’s official integrations page names **Simpro, Xero, Fergus, QuickBooks, ServiceM8, AroFlo and Ascora** [4]. The Simpro marketplace explains the workflow in concrete terms: users link their Simpro account, use existing catalogue items and pre-builds, choose cost centre/labour rate/customer/site, and push quantities and pricing into a new or existing Simpro quote [9]. This is stronger evidence than a logo wall because it identifies the handoff and required objects.

The Simpro route is a real marketplace/partnership channel, not an assumed audience. Groundplan has a listed partner page available in Australia, Canada, New Zealand, the UK/Ireland and the United States [9]. Access difficulty for a new entrant should be rated **medium-to-high**: a marketplace listing is technically accessible only after building and supporting a credible integration, and distribution depends on partner approval, customer value and ongoing reliability. The listing itself does not disclose impressions, leads or conversion, so no reach estimate should be invented. Groundplan also publishes its own social and educational channels; LinkedIn showed 1,082 followers at review time [10], but follower count is not verified reach.

A second route is adviser/implementation partnerships. Groundplan’s LinkedIn page includes a public post describing Fig Advisory becoming a Groundplan partner and planning Groundplan-trained estimating/drafting staff [10]. This is evidence that service capacity and trained-partner ecosystems are part of the category’s route to market. It is not evidence that the partnership generates material revenue or that the route is open to every competitor.

The main product weakness is extensibility. The independent The Techy Side review, researched 6 March 2026, describes Groundplan as having “pre-built integrations only, no custom API,” and says users are limited to Excel/CSV exports if their system is unsupported [7]. It also reports no public API, SDK, developer documentation or webhooks, and rates integration difficulty 8/10. These are independent claims and should be validated directly before making a procurement decision, but they identify a credible wedge: a premium competitor can win complex builders or connected businesses with reliable API/webhook support, governed exports and richer data portability.

The same review flags limited pricing transparency, lack of publicly listed SOC 2/ISO 27001 certifications and no guarantee in the privacy policy that data remains in Australia [7]. These are risks and not proof of a security failure. KindAI should use them only as diligence questions and potential enterprise differentiators, not as negative marketing without verified comparative evidence.

## Customer evidence and buyer voice

Independent review evidence is unusually positive, but it is not the same as broad market proof. Capterra Australia shows **63 reviews and a 4.8 rating**, with the page displaying 100% positive sentiment [6]. Software Advice Australia also shows 4.8 from 63 reviews and identifies the product’s principal segments as electrical, plumbing, fire and security [8]. Review-platform samples are self-selecting and may be influenced by vendor engagement, so they support product satisfaction signals rather than market-wide willingness to pay.

Australian reviewers repeatedly value usability and support. A reviewer identified as a director in Australia wrote on 31 January 2025 that the “ease of marking up plans and calculating material quantities” was strong and that “the support has been next level,” while naming first-project setup as the main difficulty [6]. Another Australian construction reviewer wrote on 25 March 2025 that the programme integrated with their job-management system and was easy to use, but said the duplication process needed more explanation [6]. An electrical reviewer using it for over two years wrote on 3 December 2025, “SUPPORT IS 11/10,” while also asking for more electrical symbols and saying some symbols did not match [6]. These are useful buyer signals: onboarding, human help, reusable project setup and trade-specific symbol coverage matter.

There are also product-quality caveats. One May 2025 review said Count Assist sometimes struggled to identify the same symbol when it was rotated or at a different angle, attributing the problem partly to plan quality [6]. That is directly relevant to AI estimating: messy drawings and symbol variation create a trust and review burden. KindAI should not promise autonomous accuracy from this evidence. It should make assumptions, confidence, exceptions and human review visible.

A Reddit thread in r/estimators is not Australia-specific and therefore cannot establish Australian demand, but it is useful category voice. In a July 2025 discussion, one estimator said most measuring was done in Revu and then transferred to Excel to calculate labour and materials [14]. Another wrote that On-Screen Takeoff was useful with “borderline no excel work,” but had “a little clunky” learning curve [14]. A separate commenter said they only needed to trace drawings and take data to Excel, preferring a cheaper, simpler tool [14]. These complaints are not Groundplan reviews and should not be treated as proof against Groundplan. They do show a recurring buying tension: some users pay for integrated estimating logic; others deliberately choose cheap tracing plus spreadsheets. KindAI’s premium offer must target the former and avoid assuming every plan user wants AI.

The strongest Groundplan customer implication is therefore **workflow confidence**, not “AI demand.” Buyers appear to value a tool that is easy to learn, backed by reachable support, reusable after initial setup, and connected to their existing job-management system. A KindAI sales motion should prove those same operational outcomes while differentiating on reviewable AI assistance and a more extensible workflow.

## Australian market context and demand discipline

Master Builders Australia reports **462,939 construction businesses** operating across Australia, **$324.6 billion** in building and construction work completed in the year to June 2025, **1.32 million** people employed and a **11.7% direct contribution to GDP** [5]. It says more than **98.6%** of construction businesses employ fewer than 20 people [5]. These figures establish a large, highly fragmented total addressable business population and explain why trade-specific, low-friction software can be commercially relevant.

They do **not** establish the market size for estimating software. The ABS’s latest Counts of Australian Businesses release, published 18 August 2026, reports **2,814,778 actively trading Australian businesses** at 30 June 2026 and a **3.4% annual increase in construction businesses** in 2025–26 [11]. ABS business counts are an economy-wide denominator, not a count of firms that perform digital takeoffs or would buy estimating software. The report therefore uses construction-business counts only as context and does not convert them into a software TAM without verified penetration, spend or buyer-intent data.

Search-demand evidence is similarly limited. Google Trends’ Australia explore page returned no readable trend data in this research environment [12], and no reliable current volume figure for “Groundplan,” “AI estimating software Australia” or “construction takeoff software Australia” was obtained. The honest conclusion is **low/unknown verifiable search demand**, not zero demand. Groundplan’s own adoption claims, its 63-review sample and its partner integrations are stronger evidence of existing category usage than a search-volume assumption. KindAI should validate demand through qualified demos, partner referrals and paid conversion rather than infer it from generic construction-industry size.

## Route-to-market relevance for KindAI

Groundplan demonstrates three routes worth testing:

1. **Integration marketplaces.** Simpro’s official marketplace gives Groundplan a discoverable, contextual route to trade businesses already using the job-management platform [9]. KindAI should treat equivalent partnerships as a build-and-approval project, not as free reach. Integration reliability and catalogue/price-book mapping are likely the entry requirements.
2. **Trade-specialist content and training.** Groundplan combines trade landing pages, guides, webinars/academy-style training and free expert onboarding [1] [3] [13]. This supports a high-intent education funnel: show an electrician, plumber or builder how a representative Australian plan becomes a quote, then let them review exceptions.
3. **Adviser and estimating-capacity partners.** The public Fig Advisory partnership post indicates that trained service providers can extend product adoption [10]. For KindAI, a small number of estimator/adviser partners could be more valuable than broad untargeted acquisition, but no reach or conversion should be assumed until measured.

The route-to-market implication is to sell through the buyer’s current system and trusted operator, not only through a generic “AI estimator” search term. Groundplan’s category language is concrete and trade-native. KindAI should use similarly concrete messages—plan revision control, quantities, assumptions, labour/material mapping, review workflow and export—rather than broad AI claims.

## Weaknesses and risks to carry into the decision

- **Price anchoring:** The A$99 marketplace price (dated July 2025) is far below KindAI’s proposed A$149 entry tier and dramatically below Pro/Mid-Tier Builder. Premium pricing needs a quantified commercial outcome and proof path, not just more AI usage.
- **Free-trial expectation:** Groundplan offers seven days of unrestricted access, free training and an expert demo [2]. Removing KindAI’s A$9 trial is defensible, but a paid or bounded evaluation must feel safer and more useful than a hard paywall.
- **Accuracy and drawing quality:** Independent and user evidence mentions symbol-recognition issues under rotation/angle and plan-quality limitations [6]. AI should be positioned as reviewable assistance with exception handling, not as an unsupported accuracy guarantee.
- **Integration gap:** Groundplan’s closed integration model is a potential wedge, but only if KindAI can actually provide stable exports/API/webhooks and maintain Australian accounting/job-management mappings [7].
- **Sample bias:** 63 review-platform reviews and vendor adoption numbers are encouraging but self-selected or self-reported. They cannot establish category-wide demand or willingness to pay.
- **Search uncertainty:** Current verifiable Australian search demand for Groundplan and generic estimating terms was not obtained. Do not build a forecast from invented or unverified keyword volumes.
- **Segment breadth:** Groundplan serves many trades. KindAI should not imitate this breadth at launch; choose one segment where AI reviewability and estimate throughput create a measurable premium.

## Specific decision for KindAI

Groundplan supports proceeding with a premium, founder-assisted KindAI pilot **only if the pilot is sold around a measurable estimating outcome**, not around generic AI access. A sensible entry offer would be a short, guided evaluation of one representative Australian plan and one complete reviewable estimate, with a small fixed number of AI runs, explicit assumptions/exceptions, export into the buyer’s existing workflow and a conversion conversation at the end. This preserves scarce AI usage while answering the buyer’s real question: “Can I trust and use the output in my quoting process?”

Groundplan does not justify claiming that KindAI can charge its configured A$149/A$450/A$1,499 tiers, nor that buyers will switch. Those prices should be treated as hypotheses to test against a narrow builder/trade segment. The competitor evidence supports three testable differentiators: **reviewable AI rather than manual-only takeoff; integration/extensibility beyond a closed pre-built list; and premium support that reduces onboarding friction.** The pilot should measure completed estimates, human correction rate, time-to-first-usable-output, export success and willingness to continue paying. Until those are observed with real Australian buyers, the correct status is commercial hypothesis—not traction or product-market fit.
